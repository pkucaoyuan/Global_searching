## Part 1: Reader Expectations (Gopen & Swan)

### 1.1 Core Thesis
- Scientific writing is hard to read NOT because of complex concepts
- Difficulty stems from **structural problems** that violate reader expectations
- **Complexity of thought need not lead to impenetrability of expression**

### 1.2 Subject-Verb Proximity
| Principle | Readers expect subject followed immediately by verb |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Problem | 20+ words between subject and verb forces re-reads |
| Solution | Move subject and verb close; relocate or remove interrupting material |

**Bad Example:**
> "The smallest of the URF's (URFA6L), a 207-nucleotide (nt) reading frame overlapping out of phase the NH2-terminal portion of the adenosinetriphosphatase (ATPase) subunit 6 gene **has been identified**..."

**Good Example:**
> "The smallest of the URF's (URFA6L) **has been identified** as..."

### 1.3 Topic Position (Sentence Beginning)
| Function | Place familiar ("old") information |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Purpose | Provides context, linkage backward |
| Effect | Reader knows whose story is being told |

### 1.4 Stress Position (Sentence End)
| Function | Place new, emphasis-worthy information |
|----------|---------------------------------------|
| Purpose | Readers naturally emphasize material at end |
| Metaphor | "Mental breath" - exhale produces emphasis |

**Key Quote:** "We do not start with strawberry shortcake and work our way up to the broccoli"

### 1.5 The #1 Problem in Professional Writing
**Misplacement of old and new information**
- Writers rush to record new information first
- Contextualizing material produced afterward
- **Writers attend to their own need to unburden rather than reader's need to receive**

### 1.6 Seven Structural Principles
1. Follow grammatical subject immediately with its verb
2. Place "new information" for emphasis in stress position
3. Place the "story subject" at sentence beginning (topic position)
4. Place "old information" in topic position for linkage
5. Articulate action of every clause in its verb
6. Provide context before asking reader to consider anything new
7. Ensure relative emphases of substance match structural expectations

**Critical Caveat:** These are NOT rules. Slavish adherence fails. Best stylists are most skillful violators.

### 1.7 Practical Transition Diagnostics

**Topic Position Check**: List the first 5-6 words of consecutive sentences. Do they share a common thread?

| Bad (focus shifts every sentence) | Good (consistent thread) |
|----------------------------------|--------------------------|
| "**Large earthquakes** along a fault..." | "**Large earthquakes** along a fault..." |
| "**The rates** at which plates move..." | "**These intervals** are approximately..." |
| "**Therefore... one** may expect..." | "**Therefore, recurrence** can be predicted..." |
| "**subsequent mainshocks** have different..." | "**However, recurrence times** may vary..." |

**Stress Position Check**: Is the most important NEW information at sentence end?

| Bad (emphasis wasted) | Good (emphasis on key info) |
|----------------------|----------------------------|
| "The rate decreased when **temp changed**." | "Changing temp caused decrease in **metabolic rate**." |
| "Algorithm improves throughput, **which is significant**." | "Algorithm achieves: **30% higher throughput**." |

**Logical Connector Palette** (vary these, don't repeat):
| Relationship | Options |
|--------------|---------|
| Contrast | However, Yet, But, In contrast |
| Consequence | Therefore, Thus, Hence, As a result |
| Addition | This [noun]..., Building on this... |
| Example | For instance, Consider..., Take... |

**Anti-Pattern**: "Furthermore... Moreover... Additionally..." chains signal AI-generated text.

## Part 2: Voice and Style (Sword Ch. 1-4)

### 2.1 The Three C's of Stylish Writers
| Principle | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Communication** | Remain intelligible within and beyond discipline |
| **Craft** | Think hard about both HOW and WHAT you write |
| **Creativity** | Resist intellectual conformity |

### 2.2 Techniques of Stylish Writers
- Eye-catching titles and subtitles
- First-person anecdotes that humanize author
- Catchy opening paragraphs (hooks)
- Concrete nouns and active verbs
- Numerous examples for abstract concepts
- Visual illustrations beyond standard charts
- References showing wide reading
- Humor (explicit or understated)

### 2.3 Why Academics Write Badly
- "Chain of misinformation" - everyone thinks rules are required
- Stylistic conformity offers comfort
- Jargon as "secret handshake" for insiders
- Imitation is easiest way to learn discourse

### 2.4 Authorial Voice
| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Use "I" to claim ownership | Excessive passive that erases agency |
| Vary between "I" and "we" | "Royal we" when you mean "I" |
| Develop consistent persona | Over-hedge that undermines authority |
| Quote sources strategically | Disappear behind citations |

## Part 3: Sentences and Verbs (Sword Ch. 5)

### 3.1 Sentence Variety
| Length | Words | Use |
|--------|-------|-----|
| Short | <15 | Punch, emphasis |
| Medium | 15-25 | Standard exposition |
| Long | 25+ | Complex relationships |

### 3.2 Verb Strength
| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| There is evidence that... | Evidence shows... |
| The study was an examination of... | The study examined... |
| A determination was made... | We determined... |
| Implementation occurred | The team installed... |

### 3.3 Things to Avoid
- Monotonous sentence patterns
- Piling prepositions ("of the impact of the effect of...")
- Beginning with "It is" or "There are"
- Burying main verb under qualifiers
- "Lard" words that add no meaning

## Part 4: Titles and Openings (Sword Ch. 6-7)

### 4.1 Title Strategies
| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| Compound (colon) | "Dancing with Professors: The Trouble with Academic Prose" |
| Question | "Can Great Books Teach Character?" |
| Provocative claim | Bold statement to spark interest |
| Concrete imagery | Specific images stick in memory |

### 4.2 Effective Hooks
1. **Story Hook**: Begin with narrative/anecdote
2. **Question Hook**: Compelling question activates curiosity
3. **Problem Hook**: Present puzzle or paradox
4. **Startling Fact**: Surprising statistic
5. **Scene-Setting**: Vivid time/place/context

### 4.3 "Sinkers" to Avoid
- "Much has been written about..." (Literature Review Opening)
- "X can be defined as..." (Definition Opening)
- "This paper will examine..." (Scope Statement Opening)
- Multiple vague sentences (Throat-Clearing Opening)
- Technical terms without context (Jargon-Dense Opening)

## Part 5: Narrative and Examples (Sword Ch. 8-9)

### 5.1 The Story Net
- All research tells a story
- Narrative elements anchor abstract concepts in human experience
- Create characters readers care about
- Build narrative tension: pose problem, anticipate solution

### 5.2 Show, Don't Tell
| Abstract/Weak | Concrete/Strong |
|---------------|-----------------|
| "Implementation occurred" | "The team installed the software" |
| "There was an increase" | "Sales jumped 40%" |
| "Research suggests" | "Smith (2010) found that..." |
| "Various factors" | "Cost, time, and expertise" |

### 5.3 Dual Coding Theory (Paivio)
Information presented both verbally and visually is better retained.
- Use photographs, diagrams, facsimiles
- Include visual elements beyond standard charts
- Connect abstract concepts to familiar experiences via analogies

## Part 6: Jargon (Sword Ch. 10)

### 6.1 The Three Types
| Type | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Necessary technical terms | No adequate substitutes | Keep, define on first use |
| Fashionable buzzwords | Signal theoretical allegiance | Eliminate |
| Nominalized abstractions | Verbs turned to nouns that drain energy | Convert back to verbs |

### 6.2 The Grandmother Test
Could an educated non-specialist understand this sentence?

### 6.3 Jargon Examples to Avoid
- "The interpellation of the subject within hegemonic discourse"
- "Problematizing the epistemological framework"
- Theoretical name-dropping (Foucault, Derrida)
- "Utilize" instead of "use"
- "Concerning" instead of "about"

## Part 7: Structure (Sword Ch. 11)

### 7.1 Common Patterns
| Pattern | Use Case |
|---------|----------|
| IMRAD | Empirical sciences |
| Problem-Solution | State problem, failed solutions, new approach |
| Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis | Argumentative writing |
| Chronological | Events over time |
| Topical/Thematic | Organized by key themes |

### 7.2 Structural Techniques
- Use **informative headings**: Not "Methods" but "Measuring Student Engagement"
- Create clear **roadmap** in opening paragraphs
- Use **transitions** to connect sections explicitly
- Vary **paragraph length**

### 7.3 The Paragraph Unit
| Position | Function |
|----------|----------|
| Opening | Establish topic + connection to previous |
| Body | Develop, illustrate, complicate main point |
| Closing | Transition or provide closure |

## Part 8: References and Big Picture (Sword Ch. 12-14)

### 8.1 Citation Practices
- Citations shape academic identity
- Footnotes have cultural and cognitive value
- Integrate sources stylishly, not mechanically

### 8.2 Situating Research
- Connect specific findings to larger significance
- Write for wider audiences
- Frame your contribution in broader context

### 8.3 The Creative Touch
- Experiment with form
- Playfulness is acceptable in academic writing
- Draw on creative writing techniques
- Consider unconventional structures

## Part 9: Quick Reference Tables

### 9.1 Structural Locations (Gopen & Swan)
| Location | Reader Expectation | What to Place |
|----------|-------------------|---------------|
| Topic Position | Perspective, context | Old info, story subject |
| Subject-Verb Gap | Should be minimal | Brief qualifiers only |
| Stress Position | Emphasis, closure | New, important info |
| Verb | Action of sentence | Actual action (not "is") |

### 9.2 Revision Checklist
- [ ] Subject and verb close together?
- [ ] Old info at beginning, new info at end?
- [ ] Strong active verbs (not "is/has")?
- [ ] Concrete nouns (not nominalizations)?
- [ ] Hook in opening (not throat-clearing)?
- [ ] Jargon defined or eliminated?
- [ ] Examples illustrate abstract claims?
- [ ] Headings informative (not generic)?
- [ ] Transitions connect sections?
- [ ] First-person voice where appropriate?

### 9.3 Words to Eliminate
| Weak | Replacement |
|------|-------------|
| utilize | use |
| implement | do, create |
| facilitate | help, enable |
| impact (v.) | affect |
| leverage | use |
| robust | strong, reliable |
| novel | new, first |
| significantly | [specific number] |
| importantly | [delete] |

## Part 10: Macro-Level Architecture

### 10.1 Notation Engineering

Mathematical notation carries cognitive load. Good notation reduces "memory lookup" costs.

| Principle | Bad | Good |
|-----------|-----|------|
| **Semantic Mnemonics** | Use x, y, z for inventory, price, time | I (Inventory), P (Price), T (Time) |
| **Hierarchical Consistency** | Random mixing of styles | Sets: 𝒮, 𝒞; Parameters: α, β; Variables: x, y; Random: X, Y |
| **Minimalism** | Triple subscripts: x_{i,j,k}^{(t)} | Avoid unless absolutely necessary |

**Rule of Thumb**: Each subscript consumes reader patience. Justify every one.

### 10.2 Related Work as "Land Grab"

Related Work is not a literature review—it's **territory claiming**.

| Don't | Do |
|-------|-----|
| "Smith did A. Jones did B. Lee did C." (Laundry List) | "Approaches fall into three categories: [1,2], [3,4], [5,6]. Ours differs by..." |
| Vague differentiation | Explicit delta with comparison table |

**Comparison Table Strategy**: Rows = methods, Columns = key features. Your method should have the most checkmarks.

### 10.3 Defense-Oriented Experiments

Experiments must answer "why does it work?" not just "does it work?"

| Component | Purpose | CS/OR Expectation |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Ablation Studies** | Prove each component is non-trivial | CS conferences: mandatory |
| **Sensitivity Analysis** | Show robustness to parameter perturbation | OR journals: expected |
| **Failure Case Analysis** | Honest limitations | Increases credibility |

**Reviewer #2 Defense**: Anticipate "what if X varies?" questions.

### 10.4 Venue Awareness

CS conferences and OR journals have different "dialects":

| Aspect | CS Conference (NeurIPS, ICML) | OR Journal (MS, OR, MSOM) |
|--------|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Pace** | Hook on page 1 | Can develop motivation over pages |
| **Figure 1** | System architecture or concept diagram | Can be later |
| **Proofs** | Sketch in main, details in appendix | Full proofs expected |
| **Emphasis** | Algorithm performance | Structural properties + managerial insights |
| **Business Context** | Brief | Can be extensive |

## Part 11: Contribution Clarity

### 11.1 The Contribution Checklist

Every bullet in "Our contributions" should map to one category:

| Category | Question | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Model** | New problem or new formulation? | "First benchmark for LLM debugging" |
| **Theory** | New bound or structural property? | "Prove threshold policy is optimal" |
| **Algorithm** | Faster, more accurate, or more robust? | "3× speedup over baseline" |
| **Experiment** | Real data or new benchmark? | "Evaluate 26 models on 7,200 episodes" |

### 11.2 Contribution Statement Patterns

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "We propose a novel method" | "We prove that X achieves Y under Z" |
| "We conduct extensive experiments" | "We evaluate 26 models across 9 error types" |
| "We make several contributions" | "We make three contributions: [specific list]" |

## Part 12: Core Principles Summary

### From Gopen & Swan
> "In real and important ways, the structure of the prose becomes the structure of the scientific argument. Improving either one will improve the other."

### From Helen Sword
> "Elegant ideas deserve elegant expression."

> "We all have the power to change the contours of that map, one publication at a time - if we choose to."

### The Fundamental Insight
**Self-determination**: Academic writing should not be constrained by convention. Many "ironclad rules" don't actually exist. Stylish writers know conventions but choose when to follow or subvert them based on communication goals.
