# Academic Writing Principles

Curated principles from university writing centers and style guides.

---

## 1. Clarity and Precision

**Source**: MIT Writing Center, Stanford Writing in Sciences

### Principle
> Every sentence should communicate exactly one idea clearly.

### Guidelines
- Use active voice when the agent is important
- Use passive voice when the action/result is more important than the agent
- Avoid nominalization (use "analyze" not "perform an analysis of")
- Be specific: "improved by 15%" not "significantly improved"

### Examples

**Weak**: The experiments were conducted and analysis was performed.

**Strong**: We conducted experiments to validate the theoretical predictions.

## 2. Logical Flow

**Source**: Harvard Writing Center

### Principle
> Each sentence should follow logically from the previous one.

### Guidelines
- Start paragraphs with topic sentences
- Use transition words to signal relationships
- Maintain consistent subject focus within paragraphs
- End paragraphs with significance or transition

### Flow Patterns
1. **General → Specific**: Introduce concept, then details
2. **Problem → Solution**: State issue, then resolution
3. **Known → Unknown**: Start with established facts, move to novel claims
4. **Old → New**: Begin sentences with known info, end with new info

## 3. Conciseness

**Source**: Strunk & White, Chicago Manual of Style

### Principle
> Omit needless words.

### Common Wordiness to Avoid

| Wordy | Concise |
|-------|---------|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| at this point in time | now |
| in the event that | if |
| a large number of | many |
| has the ability to | can |
| it is important to note that | notably |
| in terms of | regarding / in |

### Examples

**Wordy**: It is important to note that the algorithm has the ability to handle cases where the input size is large.

**Concise**: The algorithm handles large inputs efficiently.

## 4. Hedging Appropriately

**Source**: Academic writing conventions

### Principle
> Claim only what your evidence supports.

### Hedging Spectrum
1. **Strong claim**: X causes Y
2. **Moderate**: X appears to cause Y
3. **Weak**: X may contribute to Y

### When to Hedge
- Generalizing from limited data
- Interpreting correlation as causation
- Predicting future outcomes
- Drawing conclusions beyond your study

### When NOT to Hedge
- Stating established facts
- Describing your methodology
- Reporting direct observations
- Mathematical proofs

**Tags**: #principle #hedging #claims

## 5. Parallel Structure

**Source**: Standard style guides

### Principle
> Use consistent grammatical structure for related ideas.

### Examples

**Parallel Lists**:
- ❌ The method is fast, accurate, and reduces costs
- ✅ The method is fast, accurate, and cost-effective

**Parallel Comparisons**:
- ❌ Our method outperforms baseline in speed but not as good in accuracy
- ✅ Our method outperforms baseline in speed but not in accuracy

## 6. Strong Verbs

**Source**: Nature/Science writing guides

### Principle
> Use precise, strong verbs instead of weak verb + noun combinations.

### Strong Verb Replacements

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| make an improvement | improve |
| give a description | describe |
| conduct an investigation | investigate |
| reach a conclusion | conclude |
| have an effect on | affect |
| is indicative of | indicates |

**Tags**: #principle #verbs #style
